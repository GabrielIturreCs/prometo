We all have a group chat full of "I'll start on Monday."
I'm Gabriel Iturre, a developer from Jujuy, Argentina, and I built Prometo: promises between friends, with real money behind them.

Promising is free, and that's why we don't follow through.
But when money is on the line, everything changes.
Across more than 125,000 stickK commitments, people with nothing at stake succeeded 29% of the time. With money at stake, more than 70%.
The problem is that those apps work in one country, charge your card, and they hold your money.

In Prometo you create a promise: go to the gym, study, put the phone down. You invite your friends, and each of you puts in, say, ten digital dollars.
You sign in with your fingerprint: no wallet, no passwords.
Every day you post a photo, an AI checks it, and it gets recorded.
In the end, those who fail pay those who follow through. If everyone succeeds, nobody loses anything.
And it works for challenges too: Thursday's football match or Friday's game night, where only the players put in.

This only makes sense on Monad.
A promise between friends is dozens of tiny transactions: joining, daily check-ins, confirming, paying out.
With sub-second finality and near-zero fees, it feels like a chat app, not a bank.
Users never pay gas and never see the word blockchain.
And the contract is public: nobody can touch the money, not even us.

We take a small fee only when money changes hands between friends.
And it's viral by design: to start a promise, you have to invite someone.
We start with people who already bet on their word: gyms, students and gamers in Argentina and Latin America, where digital dollars are already part of everyday life.

I built it solo during Metropolis, with my partner and my friends as the first users.
While everyone else is building payment rails, we're building a reason to use them.
Prometo: put money behind your word.
